% ---------- Abstract ----------
\chapter*{ABSTRACT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{ABSTRACT}

Autonomous sidewalk navigation for micro-mobility platforms requires perception and path planning that operate reliably on embedded, low-power hardware using only a monocular camera. This thesis presents a modular vision pipeline that evolved through four design iterations---from BEV skeleton-graph construction to template-approval planning---and provides a systematic comparison of five path-planning methods across bird's-eye view (BEV) and image-space domains. A semi-supervised teacher--student framework trains a compact SegFormer-B0 student (3.7M parameters) from OneFormer Swin-L pseudo-labels, subsequently fine-tuned on 228 human-corrected masks to achieve IoU 0.964 at \SI{14.0}{ms}, providing the segmentation foundation for all downstream planning.

On 228 hand-annotated campus sidewalk frames, image-space midpoint planning with a fine-tuned segmentation model achieves \SI{22.3}{\px} lateral center error at \SI{3.1}{ms} per frame---a $271\times$ speedup over the BEV skeleton-graph method (\SI{100.5}{\px} at \SI{841}{ms}). BEV masks achieve adequate coverage (approximately 33\% occupancy), but BEV path \emph{discovery} algorithms---skeleton extraction and Dijkstra search---are prohibitively slow (770--841\,ms), making them unsuitable as the primary planning domain. Oracle-mask experiments confirm that BEV-domain planners introduce systematic error beyond what segmentation quality can explain.  A 228-frame hand annotation campaign further reveals that teacher pseudo-labels systematically over-segment unreachable background surfaces; fine-tuning on human-corrected masks raises segmentation IoU from 0.934 to 0.964.

A template-approval planner replaces the noise-sensitive skeleton-graph baseline, reducing mean heading error by 40.6\% and halving path-source switches through fixed-arc verification with family-reuse hysteresis. A GPS-conditioned waypoint-turn planner with a dual-threshold containment safety guard achieves 0\% containment failure rate in overnight validation.

An 1800-frame accepted run sustains 100\% template success at a mean segmentation IoU of 0.954 and 9.97~FPS. The image-space primary path achieves 59~FPS on CPU; the full dual-domain architecture including BEV corridor verification sustains 9.97~FPS, meeting the 10~Hz real-time requirement for pedestrian-speed platforms. All evaluation is conducted offline on daytime campus sidewalk video from a single university environment; generalization to other settings, lighting conditions, and closed-loop deployment remains future work.

\section{Motivation}

Consider a battery-powered delivery scooter navigating a university sidewalk. The path ahead is cracked asphalt bordered by grass on one side and a brick retaining wall on the other; there are no lane markings, no curb paint, and no high-definition map of the route. The vehicle's only forward-looking sensor is a commodity RGB camera, and its only computer is a single-board ARM processor drawing under fifteen watts. In this setting, a natural question arises: how should such a platform perceive the traversable corridor ahead and plan a safe trajectory through it?

The economic and social potential of autonomous sidewalk vehicles suggests that this question is worth answering carefully. Last-mile delivery consumes a disproportionate share of logistics costs, and autonomous delivery robots may help reduce this burden while lowering carbon emissions from conventional delivery vans~\cite{starship2022}. Assistive mobility scooters could extend independence for individuals with mobility limitations. Campus shuttle vehicles could improve pedestrian flow in dense environments. These applications share a common technical requirement: the ability to perceive the traversable path ahead and generate safe navigation commands in real time, using sensors and compute that fit within the form factor and power budget of a small electric vehicle. Among available sensor modalities, monocular RGB cameras stand out for their low cost (under \$50), low power consumption (under 15~W for the full system), and compact form factor---making them a natural fit for the platforms described above.

Existing approaches to vision-based navigation face limitations in this setting. End-to-end learning policies can map raw images directly to steering commands, but they typically require GPU-class hardware and offer limited interpretability~\cite{e2e_nav_policies}. Learned bird's-eye view (BEV) reconstruction methods such as Lift-Splat-Shoot~\cite{lift_splat_shoot} and BEVFormer~\cite{bevformer} achieve impressive results in structured driving scenarios, but they generally assume multi-camera rigs and computational budgets that exceed what a single-board computer can provide~\cite{zhao2024bev}. Even classical BEV approaches that project a monocular segmentation mask onto a ground plane via homography face a subtler challenge: while the BEV projection itself produces usable masks with adequate coverage, the path \emph{discovery} algorithms that operate on BEV grids---skeleton extraction, Dijkstra search, distance-transform ridge tracing---are computationally expensive, consuming hundreds of milliseconds per frame. This cost motivates a reexamination of where and how BEV-domain reasoning should be employed in a monocular sidewalk navigation system.


\section{Problem Statement}

This thesis investigates autonomous sidewalk-following navigation for micro-mobility platforms equipped with a single forward-facing monocular camera. The core task is to map each video frame to a centered, smooth navigation path through the traversable region, producing control-ready waypoints suitable for real-time execution on embedded hardware. Within this broad objective, the thesis focuses on two open questions. First, for straight-ahead path following, which planning domain yields better accuracy and computational efficiency---bird's-eye view or image-space? Second, when BEV-domain reasoning is needed for tasks such as corridor validation and turn planning, how should BEV processing be structured to retain its metric-scale benefits while meeting real-time constraints?

The scope of this work is bounded by the following constraints. All evaluation is conducted offline on pre-recorded campus sidewalk video, isolating the perception and planning stages from closed-loop vehicle dynamics. Runtime measurements are performed under CPU-only inference to reflect the computational budget of embedded deployment. The evaluation corpus consists of six video sequences totaling over \numprint{22000} frames, supplemented by 228~hand-annotated frames with ground-truth masks for quantitative planner and segmentation comparison. No closed-loop vehicle control is exercised during evaluation, ensuring that the reported metrics characterize the perception and planning pipeline in isolation.


\section{Approach Overview}

This thesis develops a modular pipeline that maps monocular camera frames to controller-ready waypoints through interpretable stages: (i)~semantic segmentation via a lightweight SegFormer-B0 model~\cite{segformer} trained with a teacher--student framework using OneFormer Swin-L~\cite{oneformer} as the teacher, (ii)~optional bird's-eye view (BEV) projection via a fixed calibrated homography, (iii)~mask refinement and connected-component filtering, (iv)~path extraction via one of five planning methods spanning BEV and image-space domains, and (v)~temporal smoothing and control output.

The system evolved through four design iterations, each backward-compatible with the previous, tracing an arc from expensive path \emph{discovery} to efficient path \emph{verification}:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Iteration~1:} BEV skeleton-graph planner with SegFormer-B2 teacher---constructing navigation graphs from noisy BEV pixels.
  \item \textbf{Iteration~2:} BEV distance-transform corridor planner with enhanced mask morphology---replacing graph construction with ridge-based path extraction.
  \item \textbf{Iteration~3:} Image-space midpoint and distance-transform planners with OneFormer Swin-L teacher---eliminating BEV dependency for straight-ahead path following.
  \item \textbf{Iteration~4:} Template-approval planner with GPS-conditioned waypoint-turn planner and containment safety guard---scoring pre-computed arc templates against BEV corridors rather than building paths from scratch.
\end{itemize}

This work complements several active research threads while addressing gaps that each leaves open. End-to-end steering policies can achieve strong performance but are difficult to interpret and typically assume GPU-class hardware~\cite{e2e_nav_policies, viteri2024}. Dense monocular BEV reconstruction methods push accuracy but remain challenging to deploy on low-power platforms~\cite{zhao2024bev}. Classic semantic-mask corridor following is efficient in structured environments such as agricultural rows but does not explicitly expose intersection topology~\cite{orchard_seg_nav, row_nav_survey}. The approach taken in this thesis is monocular, transparent, and computationally light enough for CPU-only inference. To our knowledge, no prior work provides a systematic comparison of BEV-domain and image-space path planning for monocular sidewalk navigation, which is the primary empirical contribution of this thesis.


\section{Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item A systematic comparison of path-planning methods across BEV and image-space domains on 228 hand-annotated frames, providing evidence that image-space midpoint planning achieves 22.3~px lateral error at 3.1~ms per frame versus 100.5~px at 841~ms for the BEV skeleton-graph method---a $271\times$ speedup with $4.5\times$ lower lateral error on the tested sidewalk sequences (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).

  \item A template-approval planning architecture that replaces costly skeleton-graph construction with efficient verification of pre-computed arc templates against distance-transform corridors, reducing mean pipeline time from approximately 110~ms (v1, Table~\ref{tab:iteration_progression}) to 69.5~ms (v4, Table~\ref{tab:accepted_run}) while eliminating slow per-frame graph construction. The BEV domain is retained for corridor extraction and turn validation rather than being discarded (Chapter~\ref{ch:system_design}).

  \item A semi-supervised segmentation training recipe using a high-capacity OneFormer Swin-L teacher~\cite{oneformer} to generate pseudo-labels for a compact SegFormer-B0 student~\cite{segformer}, achieving IoU of 0.934 at 14.6~ms on CPU, further improved to IoU of 0.964 through fine-tuning on 228 human-corrected masks that address teacher over-segmentation errors (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).

  \item A multi-scale offline evaluation protocol that validates findings at three complementary scales---228 hand-annotated frames for planner accuracy and segmentation evaluation, \numprint{22679}-frame replay for temporal stability, and an 1800-frame accepted run for sustained system validation---providing evidence that trends are consistent across evaluation granularities (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).

  \item A GPS-conditioned waypoint-turn planner with a containment safety guard that verifies committed paths against the BEV corridor before execution, validated with a 0\% containment failure rate (Chapter~\ref{ch:system_design}, Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{ch:background}
% =====================================================================

This chapter surveys eight areas of prior work that together form the technical foundation for this thesis: autonomous micro-mobility navigation, semantic segmentation of drivable surfaces, bird's-eye view projection, path planning for mobile robots, distance-transform methods, skeletonization and topological extraction, teacher--student learning, and embedded real-time perception. Each area contributes a piece of the pipeline described in Chapter~\ref{ch:system_design}, and each reveals a specific gap that motivates the contributions of this thesis. The chapter concludes with a summary that connects these gaps to the two principal findings introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}.


\section{Autonomous Navigation for Micro-Mobility Platforms}

The deployment of autonomous micro-mobility vehicles on sidewalks and shared pedestrian paths represents a research frontier distinct from road-driving autonomy. Commercial efforts have begun to demonstrate feasibility at scale: Starship Technologies has deployed thousands of delivery robots that navigate sidewalks using a combination of cameras, ultrasonic sensors, and pre-mapped routes~\cite{starship2022}. In the academic literature, sidewalk-capable platforms have been explored from several angles. Viteri~et~al.~\cite{viteri2024} employ an RGB-D dual-ConvNet architecture to produce steering commands for a sidewalk-following scooter, relying on depth data to estimate obstacle proximity. Machkour~et~al.~\cite{machkour2023} demonstrate monocular deep-learning navigation for ground robots, though the system targets structured indoor corridors rather than outdoor sidewalks. Zhu~et~al.~\cite{zhu2017target} present a target-driven visual navigation approach that combines monocular depth and segmentation for indoor object following without explicit mapping. Across these efforts, a common pattern emerges: most platforms rely on either depth sensors, multi-camera rigs, or pre-mapped routes to achieve reliable navigation.

A key distinction between road-driving autonomy and sidewalk navigation is the sensor budget. Road vehicles typically employ LiDAR arrays costing thousands of dollars and GPU clusters consuming hundreds of watts. Sidewalk robots, by contrast, must achieve comparable navigation reliability with cameras costing tens of dollars and single-board computers consuming under 15~watts. This order-of-magnitude gap in available compute and sensing motivates modular, geometry-based approaches whose behavior can be inspected and tuned without retraining deep models---and whose failure modes can be characterized analytically rather than through exhaustive replay.

While these platforms have demonstrated sidewalk navigation in controlled settings, no study found in the surveyed literature has benchmarked a monocular-only sidewalk navigation system end-to-end on embedded hardware while comparing planning strategies across coordinate domains. This thesis provides such a comparison, with results presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

The segmentation models that underpin these navigation systems are reviewed next.


\section{Semantic Segmentation for Drivable Surfaces}
\label{sec:lit_segmentation}

Semantic segmentation of drivable surfaces has evolved rapidly over the past decade, driven by both architectural innovation and growing dataset scale. Fully convolutional networks (FCN)~\cite{fcn2015} first demonstrated that classification architectures could be repurposed for dense prediction. Encoder-decoder designs such as DeepLabV3+~\cite{deeplabv3plus} improved boundary precision through atrous spatial pyramid pooling and skip connections, while PSPNet~\cite{pspnet} introduced pyramid pooling for global context aggregation, holding state-of-the-art on CityScapes for several years. More recently, transformer-based architectures have achieved state-of-the-art results on urban benchmarks: SegFormer~\cite{segformer} introduced a hierarchical Vision Transformer~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} encoder paired with a lightweight MLP decoder, scaling efficiently from B0 (3.7M parameters, suitable for embedded deployment) to B5 (84.7M parameters, approaching research-grade accuracy). The general trend is one of increasing accuracy accompanied by increasing compute---a tension that is particularly acute for embedded platforms.

Several lightweight architectures have been proposed specifically for real-time inference. MobileNetV3-based segmentation~\cite{edge_optimized_seg} targets edge devices with depth-wise separable convolutions. TwinLiteNet~\cite{twinlitenet2024} provides a dual-task architecture for simultaneous drivable-area and lane detection. BiSeNetV2~\cite{bisenetv2} uses a bilateral network with detail and semantic branches, while DDRNet~\cite{ddrnet} employs deep dual-resolution pathways for high-throughput inference. Ensemble approaches using multiple backbones can further improve mIoU but at the cost of increased latency and memory~\cite{shihab2024}. The design space thus presents a recurring trade-off between segmentation quality and inference speed that must be navigated with care on resource-constrained hardware.

Sidewalk-specific segmentation datasets remain limited compared to those available for road driving. CityScapes~\cite{cityscapes} includes sidewalk class labels but focuses on European urban driving scenes captured from a vehicle-mounted perspective, providing limited coverage of the narrow, irregular walkways common on university campuses. The Robot Unstructured Ground Dataset (RUGD)~\cite{rugd} provides off-road terrain segmentation relevant to unstructured path following but does not include paved sidewalks. Mapillary Vistas~\cite{mapillary} offers diverse street-level imagery with sidewalk annotations, though at resolutions and viewpoints that differ from the low-mounted cameras typical of micro-mobility platforms. The scarcity of dedicated sidewalk segmentation benchmarks complicates direct model training and motivates semi-supervised domain adaptation strategies.

The combination of dataset scarcity and the need for lightweight inference motivates semi-supervised training approaches. While knowledge distillation has been applied broadly in segmentation, the specific combination of a high-capacity universal segmentation teacher---such as OneFormer~\cite{oneformer}, which extends the Mask2Former~\cite{mask2former} architecture to unify semantic, instance, and panoptic segmentation---with a lightweight SegFormer-B0 student~\cite{segformer} has not been explored for sidewalk-specific binary segmentation. This thesis presents such a recipe in Chapter~\ref{ch:system_design}, Section~\ref{sec:teacher_student}, and evaluates the resulting model in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

Once a drivable mask is obtained, projecting it into a metric coordinate frame enables distance-based planning; the next section reviews bird's-eye view methods for this purpose.


\section{Bird's-Eye View Projection and Monocular BEV}
\label{sec:lit_bev}

Bird's-eye view (BEV) representations provide a spatially consistent top-down reference frame that simplifies metric reasoning for path planning and obstacle avoidance. The classical approach, Inverse Perspective Mapping (IPM), originally proposed for optical-flow simplification in autonomous driving~\cite{mallot1991ipm}, uses a planar homography to warp the camera image onto the ground plane~\cite{hartley_zisserman}. Bertozzi and Broggi~\cite{bertozzi1998gold} applied IPM to real-time road navigation in the GOLD system, demonstrating that the technique could support lane detection and obstacle avoidance on production vehicles at highway speeds. IPM requires a flat-surface assumption and known camera extrinsics; these assumptions hold approximately for level sidewalks but degrade on ramps, curbs, and uneven terrain. Despite these limitations, IPM remains widely used in embedded systems because it is computationally inexpensive and introduces no learned parameters.

Recent work has moved toward learned BEV representations that relax the flat-ground assumption. Lift, Splat, Shoot (LSS)~\cite{lift_splat_shoot} encodes images from arbitrary camera rigs by predicting per-pixel depth distributions and implicitly unprojecting features into a 3D voxel grid, which is then collapsed into a BEV plane. BEVFormer~\cite{bevformer} employs spatiotemporal transformers to aggregate multi-camera features into BEV space, achieving strong performance on nuScenes and Waymo benchmarks. Focus on BEV~\cite{zhao2024bev} introduces self-calibrated cycle view transformation for monocular BEV segmentation, reducing the multi-camera requirement. However, all of these learned approaches require substantial GPU compute and memory---resources that exceed the budget of a single-board ARM computer. They are designed for full-size vehicles with multi-camera sensor suites, making direct application to single-camera micro-mobility platforms impractical. Monocular depth estimation models such as MiDaS~\cite{midas2022} offer an alternative path to metric-free BEV construction without multi-camera rigs, but they introduce additional inference cost and produce relative rather than absolute depth, complicating the metric-scale reasoning that BEV planning relies upon.

A less-studied limitation of monocular BEV is \emph{computational cost}. A single forward-facing camera maps only a narrow trapezoidal strip of the ground plane into the BEV grid, yet with adequate segmentation quality, this strip achieves substantial occupancy (approximately 33\% in the experiments reported in this thesis). The real bottleneck is not mask coverage but the cost of path \emph{discovery} algorithms---skeletonization, Dijkstra search, and distance-transform ridge extraction---that operate on the BEV grid. These algorithms are inherently expensive on pixel grids, consuming hundreds of milliseconds per frame and violating real-time constraints for embedded deployment.

While BEV representations offer clear advantages for metric-scale reasoning---particularly for corridor extraction and turn planning---the cost--benefit trade-off of BEV-domain path planning versus BEV-domain path verification has not been systematically characterized for sidewalk navigation. This thesis provides such a characterization, documenting a two-orders-of-magnitude cost difference between BEV path discovery (380--927\,ms) and BEV path verification (3--5\,ms) and demonstrating that the latter retains the metric-scale benefits essential for turn geometry (Section~\ref{sec:bev_fragility_results}).

With a metric occupancy representation available, the next section reviews the path planning algorithms that operate on it.


\section{Path Planning for Mobile Robots}
\label{sec:lit_planning}

Local path planning for mobile robots encompasses a broad spectrum of methods spanning classical graph search, sampling-based techniques, reactive strategies, and learned policies. Grid-based planners such as A* and Dijkstra operate on discretized cost maps and guarantee optimal paths within the grid resolution~\cite{thrun2005probabilistic, lavalle2006}; they are well suited to 2D occupancy grids but scale poorly to high-dimensional configuration spaces. Sampling-based methods (RRT, PRM) handle dimensionality more gracefully but are less relevant for the 2D mask corridors typical of sidewalk segmentation. Trajectory optimization in a Frenet frame~\cite{werling2010frenet} generates candidate trajectories from a parameterized family of lateral and longitudinal polynomials, achieving real-time performance on structured roads; however, the Frenet formulation assumes a well-defined reference path, which is unavailable in the segmentation-only setting considered here. Potential field methods represent the environment as a superposition of attractive and repulsive forces, providing smooth reactive control~\cite{khatib1986}, though they are susceptible to local minima in complex geometries. The Dynamic Window Approach (DWA)~\cite{dwa_fox1997} plans in velocity space, sampling admissible linear and angular velocity pairs and selecting the trajectory that best balances goal heading, obstacle clearance, and forward progress; DWA is the default local planner in the widely used ROS navigation stack~\cite{quigley2009ros} and represents the standard baseline for reactive mobile-robot navigation. End-to-end steering policies trained via imitation learning or reinforcement learning bypass explicit environment representation and map directly from sensory input to control commands~\cite{e2e_nav_policies, bojarski2016}; however, they typically require GPU-class hardware and offer limited interpretability, making failure analysis difficult.

Agricultural row-following provides perhaps the closest analog to sidewalk corridor navigation. Histogram-of-column methods---which identify the densest vertical strip in a segmented mask---are fast and reliable in geometrically regular environments such as crop rows~\cite{orchard_seg_nav, row_nav_survey}. Sidewalks, however, lack the strict geometric regularity of agricultural corridors: they curve, narrow, branch at intersections, and change surface material without warning. These variations demand planners that can adapt to irregular corridor geometry while remaining computationally light.

One open question in the existing literature concerns the choice of planning domain. A comprehensive survey of road detection methods~\cite{hillel2014road} covers both image-space and world-frame approaches, yet most prior work on sidewalk and robot navigation operates in BEV or world-frame coordinates, implicitly assuming that metric-scale reasoning is necessary for accurate path extraction. The alternative of planning directly in image-space---bypassing the BEV projection entirely---has received little systematic investigation. Image-space planning avoids the computational cost and coordinate-transform errors inherent in BEV-domain path discovery but sacrifices metric scale, raising questions about whether the trade-off is worthwhile for straight-ahead corridor following.

To our knowledge, no prior study in the surveyed literature has provided a systematic comparison of BEV-domain and image-space path planning methods for monocular sidewalk navigation. This thesis introduces such a comparison across five methods in two coordinate domains, with results suggesting that image-space planning offers substantial advantages for straight-ahead following on the tested campus sequences (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).

Distance transform methods offer an alternative to graph-based planning and are reviewed in the following section.


\section{Distance Transform Methods in Navigation}

The Euclidean distance transform (EDT) computes, for each pixel in a binary mask, the distance to the nearest non-traversable pixel~\cite{felzenszwalb_dt}. The result is a clearance map in which each traversable pixel carries a scalar value proportional to its distance from the nearest boundary. The ridge of this map---the set of local maxima---traces the centerline of the traversable corridor, representing the path of maximum clearance. This property makes the EDT a natural foundation for safe corridor extraction in mobile robotics. The Euclidean Signed Distance Field (ESDF) extends the concept to three dimensions, supporting clearance-aware planning for aerial vehicles and manipulator arms.

In 2D mask planning, the EDT ridge can be traced via dynamic programming or Dijkstra search on a cost field $c(x,y) = 1/(d(x,y) + \varepsilon)^\alpha$, where $d$ is the EDT value and $\varepsilon$ prevents division by zero. This formulation naturally steers the path toward the center of wide corridors while permitting traversal of narrow passages at higher cost. Compared to morphological skeletonization, EDT-based ridge tracing is less sensitive to small mask protrusions, since the distance field smooths out local boundary noise. The approach has been employed in diverse robotic contexts, from indoor corridor following to outdoor terrain navigation.

Prior applications of distance-transform methods in navigation have used the EDT for path \emph{discovery}---extracting a trajectory from the clearance field by tracing the ridge or running graph search over the cost grid. This thesis introduces an alternative use: employing the distance-transform corridor as a \emph{verification surface} against which pre-computed template arcs are scored, shifting the planning paradigm from discovery to approval (Chapter~\ref{ch:system_design}).

A related class of methods extracts paths through topological skeletonization, reviewed next.


\section{Skeletonization and Topological Path Extraction}

Morphological skeletonization reduces a binary region to its 1-pixel-wide medial axis while preserving topological structure---branches, junctions, and connectivity. The Zhang--Suen algorithm~\cite{zhang_suen} iteratively removes boundary pixels that satisfy local deletion conditions, producing a connected skeleton in a fixed number of passes. The parallel variant by Guo and Hall~\cite{guo_hall} improves speed by processing all deletable pixels simultaneously. From the resulting skeleton, a graph can be constructed with pixels as nodes and 8-neighbor connections as edges, enabling path enumeration via standard graph search algorithms.

A common problem with morphological skeletonization is noise sensitivity. Small protrusions in the input mask---caused by segmentation error, shadow boundaries, or surface texture---generate spurious branches that must be pruned before the skeleton is useful for navigation. Pruning thresholds are typically heuristic and dataset-dependent: too aggressive and legitimate branches (intersections, forks) are lost; too conservative and spurious spurs dominate the graph. Learned skeletonization approaches~\cite{flores2025skeleton} can reduce planning cost in simulation by predicting the skeleton directly from the mask, but they introduce additional model complexity and training requirements.

Beyond noise sensitivity, the full skeleton-graph construction pipeline---morphological thinning, branch pruning, graph construction, and Dijkstra search---carries a non-trivial computational cost that is incurred every frame, since the input mask changes with each new camera image. On embedded hardware with tight latency budgets, this per-frame graph rebuild can consume a substantial fraction of the available compute, particularly when the mask is large or highly branched.

While skeleton-based topological extraction is a well-studied technique, no work found in the surveyed literature has systematically compared skeleton-graph construction against simpler template-based verification alternatives for real sidewalk data. This thesis provides such a comparison, documenting that template-approval planning achieves comparable or better path quality at substantially lower computational cost on the tested sequences (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).

The segmentation quality that feeds these planning methods depends on the training strategy; the next section reviews teacher--student approaches for training compact models.


\section{Semi-Supervised and Teacher--Student Learning}
\label{sec:lit_teacher_student}

Knowledge distillation~\cite{hinton2015distilling} transfers representational capacity from a large ``teacher'' model to a compact ``student'' model by training the student to match the teacher's output distribution rather than hard ground-truth labels. The soft probability distributions produced by the teacher encode inter-class similarities and boundary uncertainties that are lost in one-hot labels, providing a richer training signal. Structured knowledge distillation~\cite{liu2019skd} extends pixel-level distillation with pairwise and holistic losses that capture spatial relationships across the segmentation map, further improving student fidelity for dense prediction tasks. For semantic segmentation, pixel-level annotation is 10--30$\times$ more expensive than image-level classification labels, making large annotated datasets difficult to obtain.

Semi-supervised extensions of knowledge distillation use the teacher to generate pseudo-labels on unlabeled data, which are then combined with a small set of hand-annotated examples to train the student~\cite{pseudolabel_lee2013}. More recent approaches such as UniMatch~\cite{yang2023unimatch} use weak-to-strong consistency training across multiple augmentation views, achieving state-of-the-art semi-supervised segmentation with minimal labeled data. An alternative semi-supervised paradigm, the Mean Teacher approach~\cite{mean_teacher}, maintains an exponential moving average of student weights as the teacher, enforcing consistency between student and teacher predictions on unlabeled data; while effective for classification, this approach is less directly applicable when the goal is to transfer domain-specific knowledge from a pre-trained high-capacity model to a lightweight student. The quality of pseudo-labels depends critically on the teacher's accuracy and domain relevance: a teacher that generalizes well to the target domain produces cleaner pseudo-labels, reducing noise propagation to the student. Domain-adaptive approaches such as DAFormer~\cite{hoyer2022daformer} use self-training with pseudo-labels and transformer-based architectures to bridge the gap between source and target domains, demonstrating that careful pseudo-label generation is central to effective domain transfer in segmentation. Confidence thresholding and morphological post-processing can further improve pseudo-label quality by discarding low-confidence predictions and removing isolated spurious regions.

OneFormer~\cite{oneformer} is a universal image segmentation model that unifies semantic, instance, and panoptic segmentation within a single architecture through task-conditioned joint training. Pre-trained on large-scale datasets including ADE20K~\cite{zhou2017ade20k}, COCO~\cite{coco}, and CityScapes~\cite{cityscapes}, OneFormer with a Swin-L backbone~\cite{liu2021swin} provides high-quality dense predictions across a wide range of visual domains. Its broad training distribution makes it a strong candidate for generating pseudo-labels in domains where dedicated annotated data is scarce---such as sidewalk-specific binary segmentation.

While knowledge distillation has been applied broadly in semantic segmentation, the specific combination of OneFormer Swin-L as teacher and SegFormer-B0 as student has not been explored in this context. This thesis employs this combination to train a student model that, after fine-tuning on 228 human-corrected masks, achieves IoU of 0.964 at 14.0~ms inference time on CPU (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).

Deploying these trained models under real-time constraints on embedded hardware introduces additional challenges, reviewed in the following section.


\section{Embedded and Real-Time Perception}
\label{sec:lit_embedded}

Deploying perception and planning pipelines on embedded hardware introduces constraints that fundamentally shape system design. Single-board computers such as the Raspberry Pi~4 (4-core ARM Cortex-A72, 2--8~GB RAM) and Rock~5B (8-core ARM, 8--16~GB RAM) offer a fraction of the compute available on desktop GPUs, with typical CPU inference throughput one to two orders of magnitude lower than GPU inference for equivalent models. Runtime optimization frameworks such as ONNX Runtime~\cite{onnxruntime} provide cross-platform model deployment with optimized CPU and GPU backends, while TensorRT offers NVIDIA-specific acceleration on Jetson platforms. Model quantization (INT8, FP16) and operator fusion can further reduce latency, though at the cost of potential accuracy degradation that must be validated per application.

The latency requirement for pedestrian-speed micro-mobility platforms is more relaxed than for highway-speed vehicles but still non-trivial. Human visual reaction time is approximately 180--200~ms~\cite{kosinski2008reaction}, and typical camera-based robot control frequencies range from 2--10~Hz~\cite{pi0_lowfreq}. A 10~Hz (100~ms per frame) pipeline is generally considered sufficient for platforms moving at 1--2~m/s, providing enough temporal resolution to react to pedestrians, obstacles, and path geometry changes. Meeting this target on CPU-only hardware requires careful attention to model selection, resolution trade-offs, and pipeline architecture.

Less explored is the gap between component-level and system-level benchmarking in the embedded perception literature. Individual models are frequently profiled for inference latency on specific hardware, but complete perception-to-planning pipelines---encompassing segmentation, coordinate transformation, path extraction, temporal smoothing, and control output---are rarely benchmarked end-to-end on the target deployment platform. This omission makes it difficult to assess whether a system meets real-time requirements in practice, since pipeline-level bottlenecks (memory allocation, data transfer, sequential dependencies) can dominate over individual model latency.

Few complete perception-to-planning pipelines have been benchmarked end-to-end on CPU-only embedded hardware for sidewalk navigation applications. This thesis presents such benchmarking, with the recommended image-space architecture achieving 59~FPS on CPU---far exceeding the 10~Hz requirement (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).

The following section synthesizes the gaps identified across these eight areas of prior work.


\section{Summary and Research Gap}

The preceding sections have surveyed eight areas of prior work relevant to monocular sidewalk navigation: autonomous micro-mobility platforms, semantic segmentation, bird's-eye view projection, path planning, distance-transform methods, skeletonization, teacher--student learning, and embedded real-time perception. Taken together, these areas reveal four specific research gaps that motivate the contributions of this thesis.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No systematic BEV-vs-image-space comparison for sidewalk planning.} Prior work on sidewalk and robot navigation has predominantly operated in BEV or world-frame coordinates, with little investigation of image-space alternatives. No prior study has compared BEV-domain and image-space path planning methods under controlled conditions for monocular sidewalk navigation. This gap motivates the five-method, two-domain benchmarking study that constitutes the first principal finding of this thesis.

  \item \textbf{BEV discovery-vs-verification cost--benefit uncharacterized.} While the limitations of monocular BEV projection are acknowledged qualitatively in the literature, no prior work has systematically characterized the cost--benefit trade-off between BEV path \emph{discovery} (constructing paths from scratch on BEV grids) and BEV path \emph{verification} (scoring pre-computed candidates against BEV corridors) for sidewalk scenarios. This gap is addressed by the BEV cost--benefit analysis presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}, which documents a two-orders-of-magnitude cost difference between the two approaches and demonstrates that verification retains the metric-scale benefits essential for turn geometry.

  \item \textbf{OneFormer-to-SegFormer distillation not applied to sidewalk segmentation.} Knowledge distillation is well established in segmentation, but the specific combination of a universal OneFormer Swin-L teacher with a lightweight SegFormer-B0 student has not been explored for sidewalk-specific binary segmentation. This thesis presents such a recipe, achieving IoU of 0.964 at 14.0~ms on CPU after fine-tuning on human-corrected masks.

  \item \textbf{Few end-to-end CPU-only sidewalk pipeline benchmarks.} Individual perception and planning components are often profiled in isolation, but complete perception-to-planning pipelines have rarely been benchmarked end-to-end on CPU-only embedded hardware for sidewalk applications. The system-level profiling in this thesis addresses this gap, reporting 59~FPS for the recommended image-space architecture.
\end{enumerate}

Gaps~1, 2, and~4 connect directly to the first principal finding of this thesis: that image-space planning offers substantial advantages over BEV-domain planning for straight-ahead monocular sidewalk following. Gap~3 supports the segmentation foundation upon which all planning methods depend. Together, these gaps define the research space that this thesis occupies.

The gaps identified in this chapter motivate the system design described in Chapter~\ref{ch:system_design}, which presents a modular monocular pipeline addressing each of these gaps through a combination of domain-appropriate planning, template-based verification, and teacher--student training.

\chapter{System Design}
\label{ch:system_design}
% =====================================================================

This chapter describes a modular sidewalk-navigation pipeline that evolved through four design iterations, each driven by a specific failure mode discovered during development and testing on real campus video. The first iteration (v1) constructed paths from a medial-axis skeleton in BEV coordinates---an approach that seemed natural given the robotics literature but proved frustratingly unstable in practice: the skeleton would change topology frame-to-frame with minor segmentation fluctuations, causing the scooter's heading command to jump erratically. The second iteration (v2) replaced skeleton extraction with distance-transform ridge following, producing smoother paths but inheriting the full cost of BEV computation---nearly one second per frame, far too slow for real-time use. The third (v3) bypassed BEV entirely for straight-ahead corridor following after the observation that, for a forward-facing camera on a sidewalk, simply averaging the left and right mask boundaries row-by-row produces surprisingly accurate paths at a fraction of the cost. The fourth (v4) returned to BEV but only for corridor verification and turn planning, scoring a small bank of pre-computed template arcs against the distance-transform corridor rather than constructing paths from scratch.

Two domains therefore coexist in the final system: BEV provides the metric-scale coordinates needed for corridor extraction and turn planning, while image-space processing offers efficient straight-ahead path following. The chapter presents each pipeline component with its design rationale---the constraint or trade-off that motivated the chosen approach---before describing the technical details. Sections~\ref{sec:hardware}--\ref{sec:temporal_smoothing} cover perception and planning; Sections~\ref{sec:object_detection}--\ref{sec:safety} cover obstacle detection, GPS integration, steering, and safety mechanisms. \section{Hardware Platform}
\label{sec:hardware}

The hardware platform imposes the root constraint from which all subsequent design choices cascade. A single monocular camera operating under a 15\,W power budget limits the model size, inference strategy, and sensing modality available to the system. The absence of a depth sensor, stereo camera, LiDAR, or IMU means that all three-dimensional reasoning must be derived from calibrated geometry---specifically, a planar homography---rather than from direct depth measurement.

The experimental platform is an electric scooter equipped with a single forward-facing monocular RGB camera (iPhone~15 rear camera, 24\,mm equivalent focal length, f/1.6) rigidly mounted to the handlebar stem at approximately \SI{0.8}{m} above ground level, angled approximately $10^\circ$ downward from horizontal (Figure~\ref{fig:scooter_hw}). The camera captures 1920$\times$1080 video at 30~FPS in landscape orientation. No depth sensor, stereo camera, LiDAR, or IMU is used. The target deployment hardware is a Raspberry Pi~4 (Cortex-A72, 8\,GB RAM) or Rock~5B class single-board computer; all development and evaluation were conducted on a workstation (Intel Core~i7-12700K, 32\,GB RAM, NVIDIA RTX~3060) with runtime measurements reported for CPU-only inference (GPU disabled) to approximate embedded deployment conditions.  Actual Raspberry Pi latencies are expected to be 2--3$\times$ higher than the reported workstation CPU times due to lower clock speeds and memory bandwidth; the 10~Hz target includes this margin.

These hardware constraints directly shape the segmentation strategy described next.

\section{Segmentation Module and Supervision Strategy}
\label{sec:segmentation}

The real-time constraint described in Section~\ref{sec:hardware}---sustaining at least 10\,Hz on a Raspberry Pi~4---imposes a strict parameter budget on the segmentation model. SegFormer-B0 (3.7M parameters) achieves sub-50\,ms inference on CPU, leaving approximately 50\,ms for all downstream processing (BEV projection, path planning, obstacle detection, and control). Larger variants in the SegFormer family, such as B2 (24M parameters) and B5 (84.7M parameters), consume the entire per-frame budget on segmentation alone and are therefore not viable for embedded deployment. The choice of SegFormer-B0 is therefore not a preference but a constraint imposed by the hardware platform.

A second challenge arises from the limited training data available for sidewalk segmentation. Small models trained on a few hundred hand-annotated frames tend to produce noisy masks with inconsistent boundaries. Early iterations of this work addressed this limitation through a teacher--student knowledge distillation framework, initially using a SegFormer-B2 teacher. Switching the teacher to a high-capacity OneFormer Swin-L~\cite{oneformer} model pre-trained on ADE20K raised the student IoU from 0.926 to 0.934 on the 228-frame evaluation set (Table~\ref{tab:seg_comparison}).  A subsequent fine-tuning stage on 228 human-corrected masks further raised IoU to 0.964 (Section~\ref{sec:finetuned_seg}). This three-stage progression motivates the supervision strategy described in the subsections that follow.

The first stage performs pixel-wise segmentation of sidewalk regions from monocular RGB input. This module is critical for all downstream steps and must balance segmentation quality with real-time efficiency on embedded systems.

\textbf{SegFormer}~\cite{segformer} serves as the backbone architecture due to its strong performance on urban benchmarks and adaptability across model scales. Unlike conventional encoder--decoder networks, SegFormer combines a hierarchical Transformer-based encoder with a multi-scale MLP decoder, achieving competitive accuracy without positional encodings or complex upsampling. For deployment, \textbf{SegFormer-B0} (3.7M parameters) is used for its low latency and memory footprint.


\subsection{Teacher--Student Supervision}
\label{sec:teacher_student}

Small models trained on limited data often produce noisy masks. To mitigate this, a semi-supervised teacher--student framework is employed. Two teacher architectures are compared across the design iterations:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Iteration~1:} A SegFormer-B2 teacher (24M parameters) fine-tuned on 300 hand-annotated frames, generating pseudo-labels on 2,300 unlabeled frames.
  \item \textbf{Iteration~2:} A high-capacity \textbf{OneFormer Swin-L} teacher~\cite{oneformer} pre-trained on ADE20K, generating dense binary pseudo-labels from up to 1,419 unlabeled frames across 10 campus video sequences.
\end{itemize}

For each unlabeled image $x \in D_U$, the teacher predicts pixel-wise logits $z_T = f_T(x)$, converted to probabilities via softmax: $p_T = \sigma(z_T)$. A confidence threshold $\tau = 0.60$ produces binary masks:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{y}(u) =
\begin{cases}
1,& \text{if } p_T(u) \ge \tau \\
0,& \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\quad \text{with } u \in \Omega
\end{equation}
where $\Omega$ denotes the set of pixel locations. Pseudo-labels are further cleaned with connected-component filtering and morphological smoothing to remove spurious isolated predictions.


\subsection{Hybrid Training Dataset}

The training set $\mathcal{D} = D_L \cup D_P$ merges pseudo-labeled frames $D_P$ with hand-annotated frames $D_L$. In frames where both are available, hand annotations take precedence:
\begin{equation}
y_i =
\begin{cases}
y_i^{\text{hand}}, & \text{if hand-annotated} \\
\tilde{y}_i^{\text{pseudo}}, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The best-performing student was trained on 1,419 image--mask pairs (699 from earlier sequences plus 720 newly extracted frames from four additional campus videos).


\subsection{Limitations of Teacher Pseudo-Labels}
\label{sec:teacher_limits}

While the teacher--student framework dramatically improves segmentation quality over a small hand-annotated dataset, the teacher model itself introduces systematic errors that the student inherits.  These errors became apparent during a hands-on annotation campaign: we manually reviewed and corrected 228 frames from four scooter-mounted video sequences (VID\_017--020), spending several hours painting and erasing mask regions frame by frame.  Every single frame required correction---not one teacher-generated mask was accepted without modification.  Three categories of failure emerged:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Unreachable surface segmentation.}  The most persistent error: the OneFormer teacher segments any region that \emph{appears} to be road or sidewalk, including surfaces visible in the background that are physically unreachable by the scooter---behind fences, across grass, or on elevated parallel paths.  From the scooter's perspective, these surfaces are irrelevant; from the teacher's perspective, they look like road.  The teacher has no concept of traversability; it recognizes texture, not reachability.
  \item \textbf{Shadow and direct sunlight.}  Frames captured under tree canopy or near buildings showed dramatic segmentation failures.  Strong shadows cause the teacher to either miss shaded sidewalk entirely (false negatives) or hallucinate road in shadowed grass (false positives).  Direct sunlight produces similar failures where overexposed concrete blends with adjacent bright surfaces.  These frames required the most manual correction effort.
  \item \textbf{Seasonal appearance variation.}  The video data was collected during early spring, when dormant brown grass visually resembles concrete sidewalk, causing boundary bleed that the teacher cannot resolve.  During summer, bright green grass would provide substantially better contrast, likely reducing this failure mode.  This seasonal dependency is a practical concern for year-round deployment.
\end{itemize}

These failures---discovered through direct, tedious frame-by-frame annotation work---motivated the fine-tuning stage described in Section~\ref{sec:finetuned_seg}.


\subsection{Human-Corrected Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:finetuned_seg}

To address the teacher's systematic errors, the student model was fine-tuned on 228 hand-annotated frames from the four scooter-mounted video sequences.  The annotation workflow was designed for efficiency: each frame was initialized with a teacher-generated starter mask, and we corrected errors using a brush-based tool with paint (add sidewalk) and erase (remove non-sidewalk) modes, plus a straight-line tool for clean boundary edges along curbs and grass borders.  The annotation criterion was deliberately strict: \emph{traversable ego-reachable sidewalk only}, not \emph{any road-like surface in the image}.  Background roads behind fences, parallel paths across grass, and benches embedded in the sidewalk were all erased.  This criterion reflects the actual deployment need---the scooter should only follow what it can physically reach.

The fine-tuning recipe initializes from the teacher-trained checkpoint and trains for 8 epochs with a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ (cosine annealing), batch size 4, and the same cross-entropy + Dice loss used in the initial training.  The built-in validation split (every 5th frame per video) monitors overfitting.

The quantitative results of this fine-tuning are reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}, Table~\ref{tab:seg_comparison}: fine-tuning raises IoU from 0.934 to 0.964 (+3.0 percentage points) and precision from 0.951 to 0.981 (+3.0 points), while recall remains unchanged at 0.982.  The precision improvement confirms that the model learned to suppress the teacher's over-segmentation of unreachable surfaces.  The predicted mask area decreased from 865K to 838K pixels on average---closer to the ground-truth mean of 836K---indicating tighter, more conservative segmentation boundaries.


\subsection{Loss Function}

The student model $f_S$ minimizes a composite loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{CE}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_{\text{Dice}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}$ is binary cross-entropy with class weights computed from the training set (upweighting the minority sidewalk class), and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dice}}$ improves mask completeness in sparse regions. Both terms are equally weighted ($\lambda_{\text{CE}} = \lambda_{\text{Dice}} = 1.0$).


\subsection{Training Configuration}

The student model is initialized from a SegFormer-B0 checkpoint pre-trained on Cityscapes and fine-tuned using AdamW with a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-5}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and batch size 4.  A cosine annealing schedule with 10\% linear warmup decays the learning rate to $5 \times 10^{-6}$ over 10 epochs.  Class weights are computed from the training set to upweight the minority sidewalk class (typical weight ratio 1:1.9).  Data augmentation includes random horizontal flipping (50\%), color jitter (brightness and contrast $\pm$0.15, saturation $\pm$0.10, hue $\pm$0.03, applied with 70\% probability), and Gaussian blur (radius 1.0, 15\% probability).  The validation set consists of every fifth frame per video (20\% hold-out), and the checkpoint with the highest validation IoU is selected.  All training uses seed 1337 for reproducibility.  Input images are resized to $640 \times 360$ and normalized with ImageNet statistics.

\section{Bird's-Eye View Projection}
\label{sec:bev}

Several downstream components---corridor extraction, template-arc scoring, and turn planning---require metric-scale coordinates that image-space processing cannot provide. A bird's-eye view transform addresses this need. Learned BEV methods such as Lift-Splat-Shoot~\cite{lift_splat_shoot} and BEVFormer~\cite{bevformer} assume multi-camera rigs and GPU-class hardware; neither assumption holds for the monocular embedded platform described in Section~\ref{sec:hardware}. A four-point planar homography, by contrast, costs approximately 0.9\,ms on CPU and requires only a one-time manual calibration. This work therefore adopts the classical homography approach, while acknowledging its reliance on the flat-plane assumption and a fixed camera pose---limitations whose practical impact is quantified empirically in Section~\ref{sec:bev_fragility_results}. The key design insight is that BEV is not dismissed but rather assigned to the tasks where metric-scale reasoning is essential---corridor verification and turn geometry---while image-space handles the speed-critical straight-ahead path following.

To transform the segmented mask into a spatially consistent top-down representation, a planar homography is applied. Let $\mathbf{p} = [u, v, 1]^\top$ be a homogeneous image pixel and $\mathbf{p}' = [x, y, 1]^\top$ its BEV coordinate. The mapping is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}' \sim H \mathbf{p}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is a $3\times3$ homography matrix estimated via four point correspondences between the camera image and a flat ground reference. The resulting BEV mask is aligned with the robot's reference frame, with the ego position at the bottom-center and forward motion along the vertical axis.

The BEV grid covers a $6\text{m}$ (lateral) $\times$ $11\text{m}$ (forward) area at $360 \times 660$ pixels, yielding a scale of 60~px/m. Calibration is performed using an interactive tool that allows the operator to select four ground-plane correspondences for any camera mounting angle. This transformation provides metric-scale reasoning but assumes a flat plane and fixed camera pose---assumptions whose cost--benefit trade-off is analyzed empirically in Section~\ref{sec:bev_fragility_results}.

The raw BEV projection requires morphological refinement before downstream planning, described next.


% -----------------------------------------------------------------
\section{BEV Mask Refinement}
\label{sec:mask_refinement}

The perspective warp that produces the BEV mask amplifies segmentation noise at far distances, where a single misclassified pixel in the camera frame maps to a large region in BEV coordinates. Without morphological refinement, these artifacts produce corridors with spurious branches and disconnected blobs that destabilize downstream path planning. Mask cleanup is therefore not optional but a prerequisite for usable corridor geometry.

The raw BEV mask often contains noise: thin spurs, disconnected blobs, and boundary artifacts from segmentation errors amplified by the perspective warp. A multi-stage cleanup pipeline is applied:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Morphological closing and opening} with configurable kernel sizes suppress small gaps and spurs.
  \item \textbf{Connected-component filtering} retains only the largest connected region touching the ego anchor row.
  \item \textbf{Enhanced refinement} (optional): flood-fill hole filling from corners to identify enclosed holes that standard morphological operations cannot reach; Gaussian boundary smoothing to reduce jagged edges; and distance-transform-based component selection that preferentially retains the component with the highest ego clearance rather than simply the largest area.
\end{enumerate}

These stages are individually toggleable via configuration flags, allowing controlled ablation.

The refined BEV mask and image-space mask serve as input to the five planning methods described in the following section.

\section{Path Planning Methods}
\label{sec:planners}

The path planning module underwent the most significant evolution across the four design iterations described in the chapter opening. The central lesson of this progression is that the planning \emph{domain}---BEV versus image-space---matters as much as the planning \emph{algorithm}. Early iterations attempted to \emph{discover} paths by constructing geometric structures (skeletons, ridges) from noisy BEV pixels; later iterations shifted to \emph{verifying} pre-computed candidates against a clean corridor representation. This section describes all five methods evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}, presented in the order they were developed.

Five path planning methods spanning two geometric domains are compared: three operating in BEV space and two in image space. All methods receive a binary segmentation mask and produce a centerline trajectory. \subsection{BEV Skeleton-Graph Planner}
\label{sec:skeleton_planner}

The skeleton-graph planner was the first approach explored (v1), motivated by the extensive medial-axis literature in robot navigation. The idea was appealing: extract the topological skeleton of the BEV mask, build a graph, and let Dijkstra find the best path. In early tests on clean, straight sidewalk segments, the approach worked well---the skeleton traced the corridor center and the graph correctly identified the forward path. However, as soon as the system was tested on real video with shadows, surface transitions, and minor segmentation noise, the skeleton became unstable: a small mask perturbation at a boundary could create or destroy a branch, causing the selected path to jump to a completely different route on the next frame. Per-pixel graph construction also required approximately 841\,ms per frame (Table~\ref{tab:planner_comparison}), consuming the entire real-time budget on path planning alone. These limitations motivated the subsequent iterations described below.

The Guo--Hall parallel thinning algorithm~\cite{guo_hall} reduces the BEV mask to a 1-pixel-wide skeleton preserving topological structure. From this skeleton, an undirected graph is constructed: $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ where each skeleton pixel is a node and 8-neighbor connections form edges. Two-stage pruning (length-based and component filtering) suppresses spurious branches. Dijkstra's algorithm~\cite{dijkstra1959note} enumerates candidate paths from the ego node $v_0$, each scored by:
\begin{equation}
C(p_j) = \alpha \cdot \text{Curvature}(p_j) + \beta \cdot \text{LateralShift}(p_j)
\end{equation}
This method explicitly exposes junction topology but is computationally expensive due to per-pixel graph construction.


\subsection{BEV Distance-Transform Ridge Planner}
\label{sec:dt_planner}

The second iteration (v2) replaced skeleton extraction with distance-transform ridge following, reasoning that the EDT ridge would be inherently smoother than a thinned skeleton since it responds to the overall mask shape rather than individual pixel boundaries. The path quality did improve---the ridge tracked the corridor center more consistently and was less sensitive to small boundary noise. However, this method still operated in BEV coordinates, and the combined cost of EDT computation plus Dijkstra search on the BEV grid remained prohibitive. The lesson was clear: the problem was not the specific BEV algorithm but the cost of doing any per-frame path construction on the BEV pixel grid.

Rather than skeletonize, this method computes the Euclidean distance transform (EDT) of the BEV mask and traces the ridge of maximum clearance. The cost field
\begin{equation}
c(x,y) = \frac{1}{(d(x,y) + \varepsilon)^\alpha}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the EDT value, $\varepsilon = 0.5$, and $\alpha = 1.5$, is minimized via Dijkstra's algorithm to find the globally maximum-clearance path. The resulting centerline is smoothed with a Savitzky--Golay filter~\cite{savitzky1964smoothing} (window size 9, polynomial order 2). This approach avoids the noise sensitivity of skeletonization but inherits the full cost of BEV computation plus the EDT and graph search.


\subsection{BEV Template-Approval Planner}
\label{sec:template_planner}

The template-approval planner (v4) grew out of a practical realization: the scooter only ever needs to go straight, turn gently left, or turn gently right.  There is no need to discover an arbitrary path from scratch every frame---the set of plausible trajectories is small and geometrically predictable.  Instead of building a skeleton or tracing a ridge, a small bank of seven pre-computed arc templates (straight plus gentle, medium, and sharp arcs in each direction) is scored against the distance-transform corridor.  This eliminates the noise-sensitive graph and ridge construction steps entirely.  The result was immediate and substantial: a 40.6\% reduction in mean heading error and 50\% fewer path-source switches compared to the skeleton-graph baseline on 220 calibrated frames (Table~\ref{tab:template_eval}).  The fixed arc bank with family reuse hysteresis produces smoother, more predictable control output because the candidate paths are inherently smooth---unlike pixel-level Dijkstra paths that can zigzag along noisy skeleton branches.  This is the planner used in the accepted 1,800-frame deployment run.

Pre-computed trajectory libraries~\cite{howard2007trajectory} establish the propose-and-score paradigm that underpins this planner: a bank of seven predefined arc templates (straight, gentle/medium/sharp variants for left and right) is scored against a corridor extracted from the DT ridge. Each template is evaluated by its containment within the segmented corridor and its smoothness. The highest-scoring template that passes a confidence gate becomes the planned path. This method trades generality for speed and interpretability: the arc bank is fixed and small, making runtime predictable.


\subsection{Image-Space Midpoint Planner}
\label{sec:img_midpoint}

The third iteration (v3) emerged from a surprisingly simple observation.  After months of working with BEV-domain planners, we noticed that for straight-ahead corridor following, the camera's perspective geometry already preserves the information needed for path extraction: the left edge of the sidewalk in the image remains to the left of the right edge at every row, regardless of foreshortening.  Simply averaging these boundaries row-by-row produces the corridor center without any BEV transform.  The initial expectation was that this naive approach would serve as a quick baseline to be improved upon; instead, it outperformed every BEV-domain method tested---achieving 22.3\,px lateral center error at 3.1\,ms, a $271\times$ speedup over BEV skeleton-graph planning (Table~\ref{tab:planner_comparison}).  This unexpected result became the central empirical finding of the thesis: for straight-ahead path extraction, the segmentation mask already encodes sufficient geometric information, and the BEV transform introduces cost and distortion without proportional benefit.

This method operates directly on the image-plane segmentation mask $\widehat{M}_\text{img}$, bypassing BEV entirely. For each row $y$ of the mask, the left and right sidewalk boundaries are identified and the midpoint $x_\text{mid}(y) = (x_L(y) + x_R(y))/2$ is computed. Rows with fewer than a minimum number of road pixels are skipped. A connected-component filter ensures only the ego-connected mask region is used. The resulting per-row midpoints are smoothed with a Savitzky--Golay filter to produce a continuous centerline. This method is the fastest (\SI{3.1}{ms}) and produces the lowest lateral center error in the experiments reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.


\subsection{Image-Space Distance-Transform Planner}
\label{sec:img_dt}

The image-space DT planner provides a more robust alternative to midpoint extraction when mask boundaries are irregular or partially occluded. By tracing the maximum-clearance ridge rather than averaging left and right boundaries, it maintains 98.9\% inside-GT alignment at \SI{140}{ms} (Table~\ref{tab:planner_comparison}), serving as a fallback when the midpoint method encounters degraded mask quality.

The EDT is computed on the image-plane mask, and the maximum-distance ridge is traced using dynamic programming with a lateral drift constraint. A per-row forward pass from the ego (bottom-center) selects the column with minimum cost (inverse EDT) subject to a maximum lateral step per row, producing a path that tracks the widest part of the sidewalk corridor. This method is more robust than midpoint extraction on masks with irregular boundaries or partial gaps but slower (\SI{140}{ms}).


\subsection{GPS-Conditioned Waypoint-Turn Planner}
\label{sec:waypoint_turn_method}

While image-space methods suffice for straight-ahead corridor following, turn maneuvers require metric-scale arc geometry that only BEV coordinates can provide. The waypoint-turn planner therefore operates in BEV, using the corridor representation to fit and validate circular arcs for commanded turns. The containment guard described in the following subsection addresses the key safety concern: ensuring that the committed turn path stays within the BEV corridor before execution. Overnight validation testing achieved a 0\% containment failure rate, confirming that the guard reliably prevents off-corridor path commitment.

When a GPS waypoint triggers a junction maneuver (left or right intent), the system activates a dedicated waypoint-turn planner that uses the BEV mask to detect and execute the commanded turn. The planner operates in five stages:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Decision band scan.} The raw BEV mask is scanned in a forward decision band (\SIrange{2}{7}{m} ahead) to detect asymmetric road openings on the commanded side. Rows where the commanded-side pixel count exceeds the opposite side by a minimum asymmetry margin are classified as ``supported.''
  \item \textbf{Support clustering.} Supported rows are grouped into contiguous clusters (forward gap $\leq$ \SI{0.5}{m}), and the largest cluster's midpoint becomes the candidate turn apex.
  \item \textbf{Target gating.} The support score (fraction of decision-band rows with commanded-side support) must exceed an acquisition threshold of 0.40 for initial lock and 0.25 for sustain. A lock hysteresis of 3 frames to acquire and 5 frames to release prevents flicker.
  \item \textbf{Circular arc path.} A constant-curvature circular arc is fitted from the ego position through the apex, parameterized as $x = R\sin\phi$, $y = \pm R(1-\cos\phi)$ where $R$ is solved from the endpoint geometry. The arc does not attempt to rejoin the corridor centerline within the planning horizon; rejoin occurs naturally as new frames arrive.
  \item \textbf{Containment gating.} The generated path must satisfy two containment thresholds: $\geq$60\% of all path points inside the corridor, and $\geq$70\% of points within the near-field (\SI{1.2}{m}). Paths failing either gate are rejected in favor of hold behavior with speed reduction.
\end{enumerate}

The overall confidence is a weighted combination of support score, containment ratio, near-field containment, and corridor confidence, used to modulate speed during the maneuver.


\subsection{Turn Containment Safety Guard}
\label{sec:turn_containment_method}

As a final safety layer, the runtime evaluates every committed control path against the actual BEV drivable mask before execution. For each sampled point on the control path, the guard measures:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Path-outside ratio}: fraction of path samples falling outside the BEV mask.
  \item \textbf{Path-outside count}: absolute number of samples outside the mask.
  \item \textbf{Minimum boundary clearance}: signed distance-transform value at each sample (negative when outside the mask boundary).
\end{itemize}

The first \texttt{TURN\_PATH\_IGNORE\_FIRST\_SAMPLES\,=\,4} samples near the ego are excluded from evaluation because the BEV bottom-edge zone exhibits mask noise when ego anchoring is disabled. When a turn path fails containment, the system falls back to hold behavior with full speed reduction, ensuring that unsafe paths never reach the motor controller.

The raw planner outputs are temporally filtered to reduce frame-to-frame oscillation, as described next.

\section{Temporal Smoothing}
\label{sec:temporal_smoothing}

Frame-by-frame inference produces unavoidable jitter in both segmentation masks and extracted paths. Without temporal filtering, this jitter propagates to steering commands, producing oscillatory control output. Exponential moving average (EMA) smoothing with reset hysteresis balances responsiveness---allowing rapid adaptation to genuine scene changes---with stability on straight segments. Three temporal filters are applied:

\paragraph{Path temporal smoothing.}
An exponential moving average (EMA) is applied to the cubic polynomial coefficients $[a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3]$ of the fitted path. The smoothing factor $\alpha$ is adaptive: higher confidence paths receive less smoothing ($\alpha \to 0.85$), while low-confidence paths receive more ($\alpha \to 0.35$). A topology-change detector resets the filter when the maximum per-coefficient jump exceeds a threshold, preventing averaging across distinct path segments.

\paragraph{Heading temporal smoothing.}
A circular EMA filter on the heading angle handles the $\pm 180^\circ$ wraparound discontinuity. The filter resets when the heading delta exceeds $45^\circ$, allowing rapid response to sharp turns while suppressing noise on straight segments.

\paragraph{Mask temporal smoothing.}
A camera-space EMA with a conservative-blend policy reduces the effective $\alpha$ when the consecutive-frame IoU falls below a consistency threshold, since a large IoU drop signals a probable camera shake or sudden scene change:
\begin{equation}
\hat{M}_t^{\text{cam}} = \alpha_{\text{eff}} \cdot M_t + (1 - \alpha_{\text{eff}}) \cdot \hat{M}_{t-1}^{\text{cam}}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 0.65$ and consistency threshold $c_\text{thresh} = 0.20$.

Beyond path stability, the system must also detect and respond to dynamic obstacles in the scene.

\section{Lightweight Object Detection}
\label{sec:object_detection}

Obstacle detection is a safety-critical requirement: the system must halt or slow for pedestrians, cyclists, and animals sharing the sidewalk. YOLOv8-nano~\cite{yolov8,redmon2016yolo} was selected for its 3.2M-parameter footprint ($\sim$\SI{6}{MB}) and sub-30\,ms inference on CPU, leaving sufficient budget for all other pipeline stages within the 100\,ms frame target. The model is pretrained on COCO~\cite{coco} (80 classes). At inference, predictions are filtered to retain eight safety-relevant classes: person, bicycle, car, motorcycle, bus, truck, cat, and dog. A confidence threshold of $\tau_\text{det} = 0.35$ balances recall and false-positive suppression.

\subsection{Monocular Distance Estimation}

Without stereo or depth sensors, object distance is estimated from the vertical position of the bounding-box bottom edge using a pinhole-camera approximation:
\begin{equation}
d \approx \frac{h_c}{\tan\!\bigl(\phi_v \cdot (y_{\text{foot}}/H - 0.5)\bigr)}
\end{equation}
where $h_c = \SI{0.8}{m}$ is the camera mounting height, $\phi_v = \SI{55}{\degree}$ is the vertical field of view, $y_\text{foot}$ is the pixel row of the bounding-box bottom, and $H$ is the frame height. The estimate is clamped to $[0.5, 20]$~m.

\subsection{Speed Modulation}

The nearest obstacle distance $d_\text{min}$ modulates the speed setpoint:
\begin{equation}
v_{\text{obs}} =
\begin{cases}
0, & d_{\min} < d_{\text{stop}} = \SI{1.0}{m} \\
v_{\text{slow}} = \SI{0.3}{m/s}, & d_{\text{stop}} \le d_{\min} < d_{\text{close}} = \SI{3.0}{m} \\
v_{\text{nominal}}, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Note that the obstacle detection module is included as a safety-critical system component but is not independently evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}; its effect is captured implicitly through the speed modulation gates exercised during the full-pipeline evaluation runs.

Obstacle-aware speed modulation integrates with the GPS waypoint navigation system described next.


\section{GPS Waypoint Navigation}
\label{sec:gps_navigation}

GPS serves as an intent-conditioning signal rather than a localization source: it indicates \emph{where} the scooter should go at junctions (left, right, or straight) but does not govern local path following, which relies entirely on vision. This separation ensures that the system degrades gracefully when GPS accuracy is poor.

The vision-based pipeline provides \emph{local} path-following. GPS provides the \emph{global} context for branch selection at junctions. A low-cost GNSS module (u-blox NEO-series, 1~Hz update rate, typical horizontal accuracy 2.5\,m CEP) streams standard NMEA-0183 sentences via USB-serial at 9600~baud. A background thread parses \texttt{\$GPGGA} (fix quality, position) and \texttt{\$GPRMC} (ground speed, heading) sentences; fixes older than 2\,s are discarded. The operator supplies a route as a CSV file of GPS waypoints. Intent is derived by comparing the bearing from the current position to the next waypoint against the scooter's current heading: a bearing difference below $12^\circ$ yields \textsc{straight} intent, $12$--$40^\circ$ yields \textsc{left}/\textsc{right}, and above $40^\circ$ yields \textsc{sharp} turn intent.  Given the 2.5\,m GPS accuracy, intent is reliable at waypoint distances above approximately 10\,m but may oscillate at shorter ranges; the temporal heading filter (Section~\ref{sec:temporal_smoothing}) suppresses this oscillation.

The final heading command blends local vision heading $\theta_\text{vis}$ and global GPS correction $\theta_\text{gps}$:
\begin{equation}
\theta = w_v \cdot \theta_{\text{vis}} + w_g \cdot \theta_{\text{gps}}, \qquad w_v = 0.7,\ w_g = 0.3
\end{equation}
When no GPS fix is available, $w_g$ falls to zero and the system reverts to pure vision control.

The GPS intent conditions the planner selection; the resulting path is converted to steering and speed commands in the following section.


\section{Steering and Speed Computation}
\label{sec:steer_speed}

A pure-pursuit-inspired control law~\cite{coulter1992pure} is adopted for its simplicity and interpretability: the steering angle depends only on the heading to a single lookahead point, making the control behavior transparent and easy to tune for the safety constraints of sidewalk operation.

The heading angle $\theta_\text{vis}$ is computed from the planned path as the angle between the forward direction and the vector from the ego to a point approximately 40\% along the path. This is converted to both a discrete command and a continuous steering angle:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item $|\theta| < 12^\circ$: \textbf{STRAIGHT} ($v = \SI{1.5}{m/s}$)
  \item $12^\circ \le |\theta| < 40^\circ$: \textbf{LEFT/RIGHT} ($v = 1.5 - (1.5 - 0.8) \cdot \frac{|\theta|-12}{28}$ m/s)
  \item $|\theta| \ge 40^\circ$: \textbf{SHARP LEFT/RIGHT} ($v = \SI{0.4}{m/s}$)
\end{itemize}
The $12^\circ$ and $40^\circ$ thresholds and the speed profile were tuned empirically during open-loop replay on sequences IMG\_1876--IMG\_1878 to balance smooth straight-line tracking against responsive turn initiation; the 1.5~m/s maximum corresponds to a comfortable pedestrian walking pace appropriate for shared sidewalk environments.

The heading angle and speed command are smoothed with EMA or rolling-window filters to remove high-frequency jitter.

These computed commands are transmitted to the scooter's motor controller as a simple ASCII line protocol (\texttt{CMD,<steer\_deg>,<speed\_mps>$\backslash$n}) over USB-serial at the pipeline's output rate.  A hardware watchdog triggers a safety stop if no valid command is received within \SI{500}{ms}.

All commanded actions pass through the safety gate system described in the following section.


\section{Safety Mechanisms}
\label{sec:safety}

The safety architecture is designed around a single principle: any individual failure mode must trigger a safe degradation rather than a catastrophic action. Confidence-gated navigation has been explored in aerial systems~\cite{richter2017safe}, where uncertainty estimates modulate planning decisions to avoid unsafe regions; the present system adopts a similar philosophy for ground-level micro-mobility. Because the system operates in shared pedestrian spaces, the default response to uncertainty is to slow or stop rather than to maintain speed with a degraded estimate. The following gates are non-negotiable:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{No-path stop:} Full stop if no valid path for $N$ consecutive frames.
  \item \textbf{Obstacle stop/slow:} Immediate stop within \SI{1.0}{m}; slowdown within \SI{3.0}{m}.
  \item \textbf{GPS loss fallback:} Graceful degradation to vision-only control.
  \item \textbf{Manual override:} Emergency stop via hardware button or keyboard.
  \item \textbf{Segmentation instability gate:} Speed cap (\SI{0.2}{m/s}) when segmentation IoU drops below threshold for multiple consecutive frames.
\end{itemize}

The metrics used to evaluate this pipeline are defined next.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The pipeline is evaluated offline using five quantitative metrics:

\paragraph{Segmentation IoU.}
Intersection-over-union between the predicted binary mask and hand-annotated ground truth, computed per-frame and averaged.

\paragraph{Lateral path-centering error.}
For each sample point $p_i$ along the planned path, the lateral deviation from the geometric centerline $c(y_i)$ of the ground-truth segmentation mask at the same row is measured:
\begin{equation}
\text{LCE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} |x_{p_i} - c(y_i)|
\end{equation}
where $c(y)$ is the midpoint of the left and right ground-truth mask boundaries at row $y$.  Reported in pixels; lower is better.

\paragraph{Mask--path alignment (inside-GT ratio).}
The fraction of path sample points that fall within the ground-truth segmentation mask:
\begin{equation}
\text{Alignment} = \frac{L_{\text{in}}}{L_{\text{total}}} \times 100\%
\end{equation}
This metric proxies navigation safety: a path that leaves the ground-truth region risks departing the traversable corridor.

\paragraph{Temporal stability.}
Frame-to-frame IoU between consecutive cleaned masks:
\begin{equation}
\text{IoU}_t = \frac{|M_t \cap M_{t-1}|}{|M_t \cup M_{t-1}|}
\end{equation}
Frames where $\text{IoU}_t$ falls below a threshold (default 0.80) are flagged as ``unstable.''  The unstable frame rate captures temporal flicker that would cause oscillatory steering commands.

\paragraph{Runtime.}
Per-module wall-clock time in milliseconds, measured on CPU to reflect embedded deployment conditions.

The software architecture that implements these components is summarized in the final section of this chapter.

\section{Software Architecture}

\subsection{Hand-Annotated Ground Truth}

A total of 228 frames were manually annotated with pixel-level binary masks across four scooter-mounted video sequences (VID\_017--020).  Frames were extracted at uniform intervals (every 32nd frame) to cover the full temporal span of each video.  Each frame was initialized with a teacher-generated starter mask and corrected using a custom brush-based tool with paint, erase, and straight-line modes.  The correction process was labor-intensive: every frame required modification, with typical corrections involving 3--15 brush strokes for boundary tightening and 20--90 strokes for frames with heavy shadow or unreachable surface contamination.  The annotation criterion was strictly ego-reachable traversable sidewalk---background roads, elevated paths, and non-traversable surfaces were erased regardless of their visual similarity to sidewalk.  No inter-annotator agreement metric is available since all annotations were produced by a single annotator; this is noted as a threat to validity in Section~\ref{sec:threats}.  The annotations serve as ground truth for both segmentation evaluation and planner comparison.


\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

For the planner comparison, each of the five methods receives the same cleaned binary mask. For BEV methods, the mask is first warped via homography. Metrics are computed per-frame and averaged. Runtime is measured as mean per-frame wall time on CPU.

\paragraph{Training and evaluation separation.} The initial teacher-trained segmentation model is trained on pseudo-labels from sequences IMG\_1876--IMG\_1924. The fine-tuned model is additionally trained on 228 hand-annotated frames from VID\_017--020, which also serve as the evaluation set.  This overlap means the fine-tuned segmentation IoU (0.964) reflects in-distribution performance; the planner comparison numbers (Table~\ref{tab:planner_comparison}) are less affected since they measure path geometry against ground-truth masks rather than segmentation accuracy.  All data was collected on the same university campus.  This geographic overlap is a potential source of optimism in the reported metrics and is discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:threats}.


\subsection{Naive Baseline}
\label{sec:naive_baseline}

As a conceptual baseline, consider a system that extracts the traversable region via segmentation but performs no geometric path planning: for each row of the mask, the midpoint between the left and right boundaries is selected, and the vehicle follows this row-by-row center without temporal smoothing, curvature estimation, or forward look-ahead.  This approach is approximately what the image-space midpoint planner implements, minus the connected-component filtering and Savitzky--Golay smoothing that make it a proper planner.

The remarkable performance of image-space midpoint planning (22.3~px lateral center error at 3.1~ms with the fine-tuned model, as reported in Section~\ref{sec:claim_planning_domain}) suggests that for straight-ahead corridor following, the traversable mask itself already encodes sufficient geometric information for path extraction, and the dominant contribution of the planning stage is noise suppression rather than geometric reasoning.  This naive baseline provides a conceptual lower bound against which the complexity of more sophisticated planners can be assessed.

\subsection{Conclusion}

The evidence presented in this section supports the claim that image-space planning offers substantial advantages over BEV-domain planning for straight-ahead corridor following. This advantage persists even with oracle masks (Table~\ref{tab:oracle_comparison}), confirming that the BEV planning domain itself introduces error rather than merely inheriting segmentation noise.

These results do not imply that BEV processing should be abandoned.  As the iteration progression in Table~\ref{tab:iteration_progression} illustrates, the v4 template-approval planner returns to BEV coordinates in a verification role, and the turn planner (Section~\ref{sec:waypoint_turn_eval}) requires metric-scale BEV geometry for junction maneuvers.  Rather, the evidence suggests that the default planning domain for straight-ahead following should be image-space, with BEV reserved for corridor verification and turn planning.

The following section analyzes the cost--benefit trade-off of BEV-domain processing in greater detail.


% -----------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Role of BEV in the Dual-Domain Architecture}
\label{sec:claim_bev_fragility}
\label{sec:bev_fragility_results}

This section examines the cost--benefit trade-off of BEV-domain processing for monocular sidewalk navigation.  The preceding comparison (Section~\ref{sec:claim_planning_domain}) demonstrated that image-space planning is faster and more accurate than BEV-domain planning for straight-ahead following.  This section identifies the specific bottleneck---computational cost of BEV path \emph{discovery}---and distinguishes it from BEV path \emph{verification}, which is fast and adds measurable value.  Together, these findings motivate the dual-domain architecture adopted in v4: image-space for speed-critical straight-ahead following, BEV for metric-scale corridor extraction and turn geometry.

\subsection{BEV Masks Provide Adequate Coverage}

A common concern with monocular BEV projection is that the narrow trapezoidal footprint of a single camera leaves most of the BEV grid empty.  Empirical measurement on the evaluation corpus tells a different story.  With the final segmentation model (\texttt{my-segformer-road}) and the calibrated homography, BEV masks achieve approximately 33\% occupancy---a substantial traversable region that is more than sufficient for corridor extraction, template scoring, and turn geometry.  The BEV projection itself works reliably; the masks contain usable structure in every frame of the 1800-frame accepted run (100\% valid path rate, Table~\ref{tab:accepted_run}).

\subsection{The Bottleneck is Computational Cost, Not Mask Quality}

Table~\ref{tab:bev_fragility} summarizes the cost--benefit profile of BEV-domain processing across the four design iterations.  The critical insight is that BEV path \emph{discovery}---constructing navigation graphs or Dijkstra paths from scratch on the BEV mask---is prohibitively expensive (380--927\,ms), while BEV path \emph{verification}---scoring pre-computed template arcs against a distance-transform corridor---costs only 3--5\,ms per frame.

\label{tab:bev_fragility}
\begin{tabular}{llrr}
\toprule
Iteration & BEV Role & Planner Cost & BEV Value \\
\midrule

The v1 and v2 iterations applied skeleton extraction and Dijkstra search to the BEV mask, respectively.  Both methods are algorithmically expensive on pixel grids: skeletonization requires iterative thinning passes, while Dijkstra on a distance-transform cost grid visits thousands of nodes per frame.  These costs, not mask quality, are the root cause of the BEV planning bottleneck.  Even with oracle masks (Table~\ref{tab:oracle_comparison}), BEV-domain planners produce higher lateral error and lower inside-GT ratio than image-space methods, confirming that the coordinate transform itself introduces error beyond what segmentation quality can explain.

\subsection{BEV is Essential for Turn Planning}

While BEV path discovery is too slow for real-time straight-ahead following, the BEV domain remains essential for turn planning.  Junction maneuvers require metric-scale distances for arc geometry: the turn planner must reason about radii in meters, entry--apex--exit distances, and containment margins measured in physical units.  These calculations are natural in BEV coordinates but would require error-prone scale estimation in image-space.  The waypoint-turn planner (Section~\ref{sec:waypoint_turn_eval}) operates entirely in BEV and achieves a 0\% containment failure rate, validating this design choice.

This parallels the approach taken by modern autonomous driving systems, which use 3D occupancy grids for high-level decision-making while performing speed-critical perception in image-space or feature-space.  The v4 architecture applies the same principle in two dimensions with a simple homography: image-space handles the fast, per-frame path following, while BEV handles the slower, geometry-critical corridor verification and turn planning.

\subsection{Conclusion}

The evidence presented in this section supports the claim that the appropriate role of BEV in a monocular sidewalk navigation system is \emph{verification}, not \emph{discovery}.  BEV masks provide adequate coverage (approximately 33\% occupancy with the final model), and the BEV projection itself is reliable---the bottleneck is the computational cost of constructing paths from scratch on the BEV grid (380--927\,ms), not the quality of the projected mask.  The v4 dual-domain architecture resolves this by using image-space planning for speed-critical straight-ahead following and reserving BEV for corridor extraction and turn geometry, where metric-scale reasoning is essential and the per-frame cost is low (3--5\,ms).

\section{Interpretation of Key Findings}

The central finding of this thesis is that \emph{simple image-space geometry consistently outperforms the more complex BEV pipeline} in both accuracy and latency for monocular sidewalk navigation on the tested campus sequences.  This result is directly measured through the five-method planner comparison (Table~\ref{tab:planner_comparison}) and the oracle-mask experiment (Table~\ref{tab:oracle_comparison}).  The inference that this advantage generalizes beyond the tested sequences is supported---but not conclusively proven---by the consistency of the trend across the four-iteration design progression (Table~\ref{tab:iteration_progression}), multiple segmentation models, and three evaluation scales.

The image-space midpoint planner achieves 22.3~px lateral center error at 3.1~ms per frame---a $271\times$ speedup over BEV skeleton-graph planning (100.5~px, 841~ms).  These results indicate that the accuracy--latency trade-off strongly favors image-space processing for straight-ahead corridor following.  The advantage persists even with oracle masks (Table~\ref{tab:oracle_comparison}), suggesting that the BEV planning domain itself introduces error rather than merely inheriting segmentation noise.  The iteration progression from skeleton-graph (v1) through distance-transform ridge (v2) to image-space midpoint (v3) and template-approval (v4) further suggests that each attempt to improve BEV-domain planning encountered the same fundamental geometric constraint, ultimately motivating the shift to image-space as the primary planning domain.

The geometric explanation for this image-space advantage is examined in the following section.


\section{Why Image-Space Outperforms BEV}

The fundamental issue is the interaction between geometric coverage and algorithmic cost.  A monocular camera with typical field of view (55--70$^\circ$) projects a trapezoidal strip of the ground plane into the BEV grid.  With the final segmentation model, this strip achieves approximately 33\% BEV mask occupancy (Section~\ref{sec:claim_bev_fragility})---adequate coverage for corridor extraction and template scoring.  However, the path \emph{discovery} algorithms that operate on this mask---skeleton extraction and Dijkstra search---must process the entire occupied region to produce a thin output (a one-pixel-wide skeleton or ridge), and this processing is prohibitively expensive (380--927\,ms per frame).  The skeleton or ridge output itself is geometrically thin, but the computational cost is driven by the mask area, not the output size.

This cost--coverage interaction is a fundamental property of monocular BEV planning, not an implementation artifact.  Even with perfect segmentation input (oracle masks, Table~\ref{tab:oracle_comparison}), BEV-domain planning remains inferior to image-space alternatives: BEV DT achieves 69.2~px lateral center error with oracle masks compared to 15.2~px for the image-space midpoint planner under identical conditions.  The BEV coordinate transform itself introduces systematic error---the homography concentrates near-field pixels into a dense region while stretching far-field pixels, producing uneven spatial resolution that degrades path extraction quality regardless of mask accuracy.

Image-space planning avoids this issue entirely.  The segmentation mask fills a substantial portion of the image (typically 20--60\% of pixels), providing dense geometric information for boundary detection and midpoint extraction.  The per-row processing naturally follows the camera's perspective geometry, and no lossy coordinate transformation is needed.

BEV projection also assumes flat ground and fixed camera pose.  On real sidewalks, curbs, ramps, speed bumps, and camera vibration violate these assumptions, introducing geometric distortion that is absent in image-space processing.

While domain choice is the primary factor, segmentation quality also plays a role, examined next.


\section{Segmentation as a Necessary but Not Sufficient Condition}

Better segmentation helps all planners, and the effect is measurable at each stage of the training progression.  The teacher-trained model reduces temporal instability by 77\% (1.46\%~$\to$~0.33\% unstable frames, Table~\ref{tab:fullvideo_replay}) and increases the template success rate by 5.6~percentage points.  The subsequent hand-annotated fine-tuning stage (Section~\ref{sec:finetuned_seg}) further reduces lateral center error from 35.6~px to 22.3~px for the image-space midpoint planner---a 37\% improvement that approaches oracle performance (15.6~px, Table~\ref{tab:planner_seg_comparison}).  This improvement is driven by precision gains: the fine-tuned model stops segmenting unreachable background surfaces, producing tighter masks that better represent the actual traversable corridor.

However, BEV planning fails even with oracle masks: BEV skeleton-graph achieves 96.0~px lateral error with perfect input versus 15.6~px for the image-space midpoint under the same conditions (Table~\ref{tab:planner_comparison}).  These results suggest that segmentation improvement has diminishing returns for BEV planning because the bottleneck is computational cost and coordinate-transform error, not mask quality.  In contrast, segmentation improvement directly and proportionally benefits image-space planning, where the mask occupies a substantial fraction of the image and boundary quality translates directly to path accuracy.

Beyond segmentation and domain choice, the planning algorithm itself affects path stability, as discussed next.


\section{Template Planning and Turn Safety}

\subsection{Why Skeleton-Based Planning Fails}

The skeleton-graph planner (v1) is fundamentally mismatched to the sidewalk navigation task, a conclusion reached only after extensive experimentation.  Three problems proved intractable:

First, \textbf{topological instability}: Guo--Hall thinning produces skeletons whose topology changes with minor mask perturbations.  In practice, this meant that the scooter's heading command could jump by $30^\circ$ or more between consecutive frames because a single pixel of shadow at a mask boundary created a new skeleton branch, and Dijkstra selected a completely different path through it.  This instability directly produces the heading jitter and path-source switching documented in Table~\ref{tab:template_eval}.

Second, \textbf{computational cost}: per-pixel graph construction and Dijkstra search on the full BEV mask require 380--841\,ms per frame (Tables~\ref{tab:planner_comparison},~\ref{tab:iteration_progression}), consuming the entire real-time budget on path planning alone.  No amount of pruning or algorithmic optimization reduced this cost sufficiently; the problem is that the BEV mask has hundreds of thousands of pixels, and the skeleton algorithms must visit a substantial fraction of them.

Third, \textbf{unnecessary generality}: skeleton extraction discovers \emph{all} topological paths through the mask---every branch, every junction, every possible route.  For sidewalk following, the vehicle only needs the single forward corridor.  The junction topology that skeletonization so expensively exposes is better handled by a simple GPS bearing comparison that tells the system ``turn left at the next intersection,'' rather than by exhaustive graph search.

\subsection{Template-Approval as the Alternative}

The template-approval planner addresses all three skeleton failures.  By scoring a fixed bank of seven pre-computed arc templates against the BEV corridor, it replaces per-frame path \emph{discovery} with path \emph{verification}.  This eliminates topological instability (the arc bank is fixed and smooth), reduces planner cost to 1.45\,ms per frame (Table~\ref{tab:accepted_run}), and restricts the solution space to geometrically plausible ego-anchored trajectories.  The result is a 40.6\% reduction in mean heading error and 50\% fewer path-source switches compared to the skeleton-graph baseline (Table~\ref{tab:template_eval}).

\subsection{GPS Intent for Turn Handling}

For junction maneuvers, the system uses GPS intent conditioning rather than skeleton-based topology discovery.  When GPS bearing comparison signals a left or right turn, the waypoint-turn planner activates a dedicated BEV corridor scan to detect the opening and fits a circular arc through the turn geometry.  This design separates two concerns: \emph{where to turn} (answered by GPS intent) and \emph{how to turn safely} (answered by BEV corridor verification with containment gating).  The skeleton-graph approach conflates these concerns by attempting to discover all possible paths and select among them---an approach that is both slower and less reliable than intent-conditioned verification.

The containment guard adds a critical safety layer by verifying the final committed path against the actual BEV mask---ensuring that planner confidence alone does not gate safety.  The 0\% containment failure rate in validation suggests the guard is effective, though it is intentionally conservative: when a turn path clips the mask boundary, the system prefers safe hold behavior over an unsafe maneuver.

The 1800-frame accepted run (100\% template success, 0.954 IoU, 9.97~FPS) demonstrates that the combined system---fine-tuned segmentation, template-approval planner, and GPS-conditioned waypoint-turn planner---can sustain stable operation over extended sequences.  However, the validation remains concentrated on a single campus route (VID\_017), and broader generalization is not yet established.

The conditions under which this approach degrades are characterized in the following section.


\section{When Image-Space Planning Fails}
\label{sec:failure_analysis}

The image-space midpoint planner is designed for straight-ahead corridor following, and its geometric assumptions impose well-defined boundary conditions.  This section characterizes four scenarios in which the approach degrades, together with the mitigations that the overall system provides.

\paragraph{Sharp turns.}
The image-space midpoint planner extracts the lateral center of the segmentation mask independently in each image row, an assumption that holds when the sidewalk extends roughly forward from the camera.  At turn angles exceeding approximately 45$^\circ$, the mask bends laterally across the image and the per-row midpoint drifts toward the outside of the curve, producing paths that clip the inner boundary.  This failure mode motivates the template-approval planner and the waypoint-turn planner introduced in Sections~\ref{sec:template_planner} and~\ref{sec:waypoint_turn_method}, which plan in the BEV domain where curvature is explicitly represented.

\paragraph{Narrow sidewalks.}
When the sidewalk width falls below approximately one meter, the segmentation mask occupies only a few pixels laterally in the camera view.  In this regime, boundary detection becomes unreliable and the per-row midpoint exhibits high variance, as small segmentation errors shift the center by a proportionally large amount.  The temporal smoother bridges brief instabilities of this kind, and a wider camera field of view could reduce the frequency of such events by increasing the pixel budget allocated to narrow surfaces.

\paragraph{Heavy occlusion.}
When pedestrians, parked vehicles, or overhanging vegetation occlude more than approximately 60\% of the expected mask area, the remaining mask fragments and the per-row midpoint extraction produces discontinuous paths.  The temporal smoother can bridge brief occlusions by holding the previous stable path, but sustained mask loss triggers the segmentation stability gate (Section~\ref{sec:safety}), which caps the commanded speed until stable mask coverage is restored.

\paragraph{Far-range distortion.}
At distances beyond approximately eight meters from the camera, perspective compression reduces the lateral dimension of the sidewalk to a small number of pixels, making midpoint extraction imprecise.  This effect primarily affects the lookahead portion of the planned path rather than the near-field region that directly governs steering commands.  The eight-meter planning horizon adopted in this system is chosen in part to limit exposure to this far-range degradation.

Despite these boundary conditions, the geometric coverage insight generalizes beyond sidewalk navigation, as discussed next.


\section{Broader Implications for Monocular Navigation}
\label{sec:broader_implications}

The geometric coverage finding reported in this thesis---that image-space representations preserve more useful planning information than BEV for single-camera systems---may extend beyond sidewalk navigation, though this has not been empirically verified outside the tested domain.  Any monocular corridor-following task faces a similar geometric constraint: a single perspective image maps a large fraction of the scene into image pixels, while a BEV projection concentrates the same information into an occupied strip surrounded by empty space.  Agricultural row following, indoor hallway navigation, and trail following on unstructured terrain share aspects of this geometric property; Aghi et al.~\cite{aghi2021local} demonstrate segmentation-based corridor following in vineyard rows on embedded hardware, confirming the viability of lightweight perception pipelines in structured outdoor environments.  The results presented here suggest that image-space planning may offer a favorable accuracy--latency trade-off for these related domains as well, particularly on resource-constrained embedded platforms, though cross-domain evaluation is needed to confirm this hypothesis.

The template-approval planning paradigm---scoring pre-computed geometric candidates against a sensory surface rather than constructing paths from scratch---represents a general propose-and-verify strategy applicable beyond sidewalk navigation.  This approach trades global optimality for computational efficiency and temporal stability, properties that are especially valuable on platforms with limited processing budgets.  The 40.6\% heading error reduction achieved by the template-approval planner over the skeleton-graph baseline (Table~\ref{tab:template_eval}) suggests that, for navigation tasks where the set of plausible trajectories is geometrically constrained, pre-computation and verification can outperform unconstrained path discovery.

The 59~FPS CPU-only throughput of the recommended image-space architecture (Table~\ref{tab:runtime_configs}) demonstrates that competitive sidewalk navigation is achievable without GPU acceleration.  This result expands the deployment envelope for low-cost embedded platforms---including single-board computers, micro-controllers with camera interfaces, and battery-constrained mobile robots---that cannot accommodate the power and cost of a discrete GPU.  The common assumption that autonomous navigation requires GPU-accelerated deep learning for all pipeline components is not supported by the evidence presented here, at least for monocular corridor-following tasks with segmentation models compact enough for CPU inference on the tested daytime campus sequences.

Several limitations constrain the scope of these findings.


\section{Limitations}

Several limitations qualify the findings of this thesis:

\paragraph{Offline evaluation only.} All experiments are conducted offline on recorded video.  The planned paths are evaluated against ground-truth masks but are never executed on the physical scooter.  Path quality metrics (lateral center error, inside-GT ratio) are necessary but not sufficient for navigation safety.

\paragraph{Hand-annotated sample.} The expanded planner comparison uses 228 hand-annotated frames across four scooter-mounted video sequences (VID\_017--020), substantially larger than the initial 32-frame evaluation.  However, all annotations were produced by a single annotator and all data comes from the same campus, limiting inter-annotator reliability and environmental diversity.

\paragraph{Fixed camera geometry.} All experiments use a single camera mount position and angle.  The homography is calibrated for this specific configuration.  Different camera heights, angles, or lens characteristics would require recalibration and may affect the relative performance of BEV-domain methods.

\paragraph{No explicit obstacle modeling.} YOLOv8n detection exists in the system but is not integrated into the planner cost function.  Dynamic obstacles are handled only via implicit mask avoidance.

\paragraph{Single campus environment.} All data comes from the University of Oklahoma campus.  Generalization to other cities, surfaces (brick, gravel, dirt), weather conditions (rain, snow), and lighting (nighttime) is unknown.

\paragraph{Conservative turn behavior.} The turn containment guard is intentionally conservative: if GPS intent remains latched after the maneuver window closes, the system prefers hold behavior rather than forcing a turn whose path leaves the mask.  This design prioritizes safety over liveness but means that stale or stuck intent can cause unnecessary stops.  The containment validation has been concentrated on VID\_017; behavior on videos 018--020 has not yet been verified.

In addition to these limitations, specific threats to validity merit consideration.


\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Internal validity.} The same campus environment is used for both training and evaluation, introducing potential geographic overfitting: the segmentation model may have learned campus-specific visual features (e.g., specific concrete textures, grass-border patterns) that inflate reported accuracy.  The 228 hand-annotated frames used for the fine-tuned model evaluation overlap with the fine-tuning training data (same video sequences with a 20\% validation hold-out), so the reported fine-tuned IoU (0.964) reflects in-distribution performance.  All annotations were produced by a single annotator, introducing potential annotator bias; inter-annotator agreement is not measured.  The temporal smoothing evaluation uses the same EMA infrastructure being evaluated, and no independent smoothing baseline (e.g., Kalman filter, moving average) is compared.  No inferential statistics (confidence intervals, significance tests) are reported for the planner comparison; the reported differences, while consistent across 228 frames and multiple evaluation scales, should be interpreted as observed effect sizes rather than statistically confirmed results.

\paragraph{External validity.} All data was collected during daytime hours under clear to partly cloudy conditions on a single university campus.  Results may not generalize to non-campus environments, different surface materials (brick, gravel, dirt), weather conditions (rain, snow, nighttime), different scooter platforms, or significantly different camera configurations.  The stride-based full-video evaluation processes every frame sequentially but does not exercise closed-loop vehicle dynamics; path quality in open-loop replay may be optimistic relative to actual deployment where steering errors compound over time.

\paragraph{Construct validity.} Lateral center error and inside-GT ratio are proxies for navigation quality.  A path that scores well on these metrics might still be unsuitable for real-world navigation if it is temporally unstable or induces uncomfortable steering behavior.  The 10~Hz real-time target is validated on a workstation CPU, not on the actual embedded deployment target; the reported frame rates should be treated as upper bounds for Raspberry Pi deployment.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

This thesis traced the development and evaluation of a monocular-camera sidewalk navigation pipeline through four design iterations---from a skeleton-graph baseline through distance-transform corridor planning and image-space midpoint extraction to a template-approval architecture with GPS-conditioned turn planning.  Through systematic offline evaluation on University of Oklahoma campus sidewalk video, five specific contributions were examined, each supported by quantitative evidence across multiple evaluation scales.


\section{Summary of Contributions}

\textbf{Contribution~1:} A planner comparison across BEV and image-space domains on 228 hand-annotated frames (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).  Image-space midpoint planning achieves 22.3~px lateral error at 3.1~ms---a $271\times$ speedup over the BEV skeleton-graph method (100.5~px, 841~ms)---suggesting that, for monocular sidewalk following on the tested sequences, image-space planning should serve as the primary domain, with BEV reserved for corridor verification and turn planning.

\textbf{Contribution~2:} A template-approval planner that replaces skeleton-graph construction with verification of pre-computed arc templates (Chapter~\ref{ch:system_design}).  On a 220-frame clip, it reduces mean heading error by 40.6\% and halves path-source switches relative to the skeleton-graph baseline (Table~\ref{tab:template_eval}), with 100\% success rate sustained across the 1,800-frame accepted run.

\textbf{Contribution~3:} A semi-supervised teacher--student segmentation recipe (OneFormer Swin-L teacher, SegFormer-B0 student) achieving IoU~0.934, further improved to IoU~0.964 through fine-tuning on 228 human-corrected masks that address teacher over-segmentation of unreachable surfaces (Table~\ref{tab:seg_comparison}).

\textbf{Contribution~4:} A multi-scale offline evaluation protocol---228 hand-annotated frames for planner and segmentation comparison, \numprint{22679}-frame replay for temporal stability, and 1,800-frame accepted run for sustained validation---showing consistent trends across scales (Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}).  This partially compensates for the absence of closed-loop deployment testing, though on-platform validation remains necessary.

\textbf{Contribution~5:} A GPS-conditioned waypoint-turn planner with dual-threshold containment gating, achieving 0\% containment failure rate at 12.9~FPS on a 300-frame turn sequence (Table~\ref{tab:waypoint_turn_eval}).  The containment guard validates committed turn paths against the BEV corridor before execution, providing a practical safety mechanism for GPS-directed maneuvers.


\section{Future Work}

The following directions are organized by proximity to the current system state.

\subsection{Near-Term Extensions}

\paragraph{Closed-loop deployment.}  The most critical next step is deploying the complete stack on the physical scooter with real-time obstacle detection, measuring actual tracking error against ground-truth trajectories, and characterizing failure modes under closed-loop vehicle dynamics.

\paragraph{Multi-annotator evaluation.}  The current 228-frame evaluation set was produced by a single annotator.  Adding additional annotators for inter-rater agreement analysis, and expanding to include frames from different seasons and times of day, would provide stronger statistical evidence and address the single-annotator limitation noted in Section~\ref{sec:threats}.

\paragraph{Cross-environment generalization.}  Evaluation on diverse sidewalk environments---different cities, weather conditions, surface materials, and nighttime illumination---would strengthen the external validity of the findings reported here.

\subsection{Longer-Term Directions}

\paragraph{Temporal video segmentation.}  Video-level consistency mechanisms such as temporal attention or optical-flow guidance could further reduce frame-to-frame segmentation flicker beyond what EMA smoothing achieves.

\paragraph{Multi-camera or depth fusion.}  Adding a depth sensor or stereo pair would enable metric obstacle distance estimation without the pinhole approximation, while retaining the monocular pipeline as the primary perception path.

\paragraph{Global route planning.}  The waypoint-turn planner introduced in this work provides GPS-conditioned junction behavior, but the GPS integration remains coarse (left/right/straight intent derived from bearing comparison).  A full global route planner with a topological map, intersection detection, and multi-waypoint sequencing would enable autonomous multi-block navigation.

\medskip

The geometric coverage insight at the core of this work---that monocular perspective images preserve denser planning information than BEV projections---applies to corridor-following tasks beyond sidewalk navigation.  The modular, interpretable architecture presented in this thesis enables incremental improvement, as each component (segmentation, planning, temporal smoothing, turn handling) can be independently upgraded or replaced without disrupting the overall pipeline.

\chapter{Algorithm Pseudocode}
\label{app:algorithms}

This appendix provides formal pseudocode for the three novel planning algorithms described in Chapter~\ref{ch:system_design}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Image-Space Midpoint Planner}
\label{alg:midpoint}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Camera-space binary mask $M_{\text{img}}$ ($H \times W$), ego column $x_{\text{ego}}$
\Ensure Smoothed centerline path $\mathbf{p}$
\State $M_c \gets$ largest connected component of $M_{\text{img}}$ touching ego band
\State $\mathbf{p} \gets \emptyset$
\For{each row $y$ from bottom to top}
    \State $\mathbf{x}_s \gets$ nonzero pixel columns in $M_c[y]$
    \If{$|\mathbf{x}_s| < w_{\min}$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
    \State Group $\mathbf{x}_s$ into contiguous runs
    \State Select best run by width and proximity to reference center
    \State $x_L, x_R \gets$ left and right boundaries of selected run
    \State $x_{\text{mid}} \gets (x_L + x_R) / 2$
    \State Append $(x_{\text{mid}}, y)$ to $\mathbf{p}$
\EndFor
\State $\mathbf{p} \gets \Call{SavitzkyGolay}{\mathbf{p}, \text{window}=9, \text{order}=2}$
\State \Return $\mathbf{p}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Template-Approval Planner}
\label{alg:template_approval}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require BEV mask $M$, previous family $f_{\text{prev}}$, GPS intent $\iota$
\Ensure Approved path, confidence, family ID

\Statex \textbf{Phase A: Corridor Extraction}
\For{each sampled row $r$ from ego upward, step 4\,px}
    \State Scan $M[r]$ for contiguous runs; select best by width and center proximity
    \State Record boundaries $(\ell_r, r_r, c_r)$; update reference center
\EndFor
\State Compute corridor confidence $\gamma$ from coverage, span, width consistency

\Statex \textbf{Phase B: Template Bank}
\State $\mathcal{T} \gets$ \{straight, left-gentle, left-medium, left-sharp, right-gentle, right-medium, right-sharp\}
\For{each template} build circular arc from ego; resample at $\Delta s = 0.25$\,m \EndFor
\If{$\iota \in \{\text{left}, \text{right}\}$} filter $\mathcal{T}$ to matching family \EndIf

\Statex \textbf{Phase C: Score Templates}
\For{each $T_k \in \mathcal{T}$}
    \State $\rho_{\text{contain}} \gets$ fraction of samples inside corridor
    \State $\rho_{\text{near}} \gets$ containment in first 1.2\,m
    \State $S_k \gets 0.22\,\rho_{\text{contain}} + 0.18\,\rho_{\text{near}} + 0.16\,s_{\text{clear}} + 0.10\,s_{\text{center}} + 0.14\,s_{\text{cont}} + \ldots$
    \State $\text{approved}_k \gets \rho_{\text{contain}} \ge 0.60 \;\wedge\; \rho_{\text{near}} \ge 0.72$
\EndFor

\Statex \textbf{Phase D: Hysteresis}
\State Add family-reuse bonus $+0.06$ to templates matching $f_{\text{prev}}$
\State Apply straight-preference and switch-margin rules

\Statex \textbf{Phase E: Decision}
\State $T^* \gets$ top-ranked approved candidate
\State \Return (path, approved, confidence, $T^*$.family)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{GPS-Conditioned Waypoint-Turn Planner}
\label{alg:waypoint_turn}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require BEV corridor $\mathcal{C}$, GPS intent $\iota \in \{\text{left}, \text{right}\}$, BEV mask $M$
\Ensure Turn path, containment status, confidence

\Statex \textbf{Step 1: Decision Band Scan}
\State Scan forward band $[2.0, 7.0]$\,m for asymmetric road openings on commanded side
\State $\sigma \gets$ fraction of rows with commanded-side support exceeding opposite side

\Statex \textbf{Step 2: Support Clustering}
\State Group supported rows into clusters (max gap $\le 0.5$\,m)
\State $\tau \gets$ midpoint of largest cluster (candidate turn apex)

\Statex \textbf{Step 3: Target Gating}
\If{acquiring} require $\sigma \ge 0.40$ for 3 consecutive frames
\ElsIf{sustaining} require $\sigma \ge 0.25$; release after 5 frames below
\EndIf

\Statex \textbf{Step 4: Circular Arc Path}
\State Fit constant-curvature arc from ego through apex $\tau$
\State $R \gets \Delta x / \sin\theta$; parameterize as $x = R\sin\phi$, $y = \pm R(1-\cos\phi)$

\Statex \textbf{Step 5: Containment Gating}
\State Require $\ge 60\%$ of path inside corridor AND $\ge 70\%$ in near-field ($\le 1.2$\,m)
\State Exclude first 4 samples (near-ego BEV noise zone)
\If{containment fails} \Return hold behavior with speed reduction \EndIf
\State \Return (arc path, confidence, slowdown recommendation)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
